This comic references a common task used to benchmark cognitive functioning in mice, speed of maze-solving. For example, if testing whether XYZ compound impairs mouse spatial reasoning (or something like that), scientists might use the compound onnmice and not use the compound onnother mice, then compare the average maze-solving speed between the two groups. In such studies, the maze is designed to be solvable with one (or more) possible routes from the start point to the finish; unlike those mazes, the one in this comic appears to be impossible, with walls completely blocking both sides of the exit at the rear of the maze.

Supposedly, these 'scientists' have been using mice in mazes for over 100 years for the main purpose of finding a mouse that can solve the maze, and they are having a press conference because they finally found one. Having the mouse solve the maze is not usually the point of the experiment; the mouse's performance is simply a convenient benchmark to test some other experimental condition.

The title text references a fictional liminal space called theThe Backrooms, which is known for being infinite and maze-like. In 2026, the filmBackroomswas released based on this "mythology". In this case, relative to a mouse, a moderately-sized maze for a human would be labyrinthian to a mouse, thereby creating the impression of an infinite maze. It is common for many human "mythologies" or stories to be based on a fear of the unknown or of extrapolations of real phenomena; in this case the Backrooms are based on the unsettling phenomenon of liminal spaces and the familiar human fear of getting lost in an unfamiliar place. The title text implies that mice, because of their own experiences with mazes, have also developed their own Backrooms-like "mythology". Mice have not been known to do this in real life.[citation needed]
