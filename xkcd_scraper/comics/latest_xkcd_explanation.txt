This comic purports to be a scene from the TV showAntiques Roadshow, aBBCandPBSseries in which members of the public can haveantiquesassessed and valued by experts. There are two common outcomes of the valuation: the item is a genuine antique of surprisingly high value, or the item is a modern replica and of a disappointingly low value. This comic shows the unusual scenario ofCueballhaving astarappraised — an item that doesn't normally get valued on the show and would not normally fit on a table of that size.[citation needed]

Stars are normally giant balls of stellar plasma that survive for billions of years (in this sense they would qualify as "antiques"); in typical xkcd fashion, some magical realism has made the stars tiny enough to be owned by people. After meeting theRoadshowexperts, Cueball learns his star is not, as he had hoped, an oldpopulation III star, but a more recentpopulation I star, as revealed by its iron content. Younger stars tend to have higher levels of iron, while older population III stars are composed almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. Cueball's comment "at least it has sentimental value" is similar to things people on the show say after learning that their "antique" is in fact a modern, low-value replica.

Incosmology,stellar populationgroups stars into three broad categories based on their chemical composition, which is correlated with when the stars formed during the history of the universe. Population III stars, which were the first stars to form after theBig Bang, have never been directly observed. It is thought that they were very large, and formed out of gas that contained no heavy elements such as iron. As they were large, they burned their fuel rapidly and exploded insupernovae, seeding the universe with the first elements heavier than hydrogen and helium. The direct observation of a population III star would be a major result in astronomy, and hence very valuable in scientific terms. Population I stars are much more common; all the stars visible in the sky, including the Sun, are population I stars. Despite being cosmologically later in evolution, these stars can be billions of years old, older than any object assessed by the Roadshow.

The title text about "family lore" echoes other things that happen inAntiques Roadshow, with people who are having items assessed often having family stories about the items. The "star-forming gas cloud" refers to clouds of gas and dust calledmolecular cloudsthat are thecommon birthplaces of stars. Stars can get ejected from them via a gravitational slingshot from other star(s). The "galactic thin disk" is a part of a spiral galaxy that contains most of the galaxy's stars.
