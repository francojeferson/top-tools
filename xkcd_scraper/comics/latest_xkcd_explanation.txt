In this comic, theDaytona International Speedway, a track for car races, is compared with various particle accelerators, even though the two are completely different things.

"eV" stands for "electron Volt", a non-SI unit of energy. It's typically used inparticle physics, where exceedingly small & light particles are accelerated to exceedingly high velocities approaching the speed of light. Trying to increase the resulting energy leads to ever larger machines, having circumferences of dozens of kilometers, necessary because of the energy losses as the particles are turned at those speeds. This also makes them far more expensive. Judging by collision energy alone, a racecar is a far cheaper way to cause a collision than a particle accelerator (and it has a shorter track).

At first glance, it may seem remarkable that a simple raceway produces collisions with energies so much higher than those of the various particle colliders, by about 24 orders of magnitude. It's important to note that the comparison needs to take into account the masses of the colliding objects. A vehicle might have a mass of 1000 kg; ( a current Generation6 vehicle is specified for a minimum operational mass of 1542 kg) . The Large Hadron Collider operates on particles ranging from protons (1 atomic mass unit, 1.66 x 10-27kg) to lead atom nuclei (204 to 208 AMUs, 3.388 x 10-25to 3.45 x 10-25kg). The particle colliders provide much higher (energy/mass) values.

In a cyclotron, introducing antimatter circling in the opposite direction would allow more energy to be put into the collision products because the original particles are destroyed (at least in theory; withbaryons, it is often the case that only some of thequarks/antiquarks annihilate), so the equivalent energy of their rest mass—and, more significantly, their remaining kinetic energy after the collision—does not detract from the energy budget available to create collision products. This does not matter as much as people think it does, but it would lead to audience excitement because of pure energy, i.e. light, being emitted.

Introducing a hypothetical antimatter race car going in the wrong direction thus not only results in even more carnage but in spectacular fireworks as well. The fact that you, as the spectator in the stands, would not survive such an energy blast (the Hiroshima atomic blast released the energy equivalent to only a few grams of matter) is typical of XKCD.
