This comic is a math-themed parody ofsoftware security vulnerabilities— flaws within a piece of software (such as an app or website) which can be exploited by hackers. Security vulnerabilities come in many forms and their severity varies, from exposure of sensitive user data to catastrophic software bugs which allowremote code executionon a vulnerable system. xkcd, as is typical, riffs on this by treatingnumbersas something susceptible to security vulnerabilities. This is reminiscent of3137: Cursed Number. System administrators will often be advised to apply a patch to fix such problems as soon as possible, in order to prevent exploitation. In severe cases the advice may be to make the system unavailable until fixed.

Here it is claimed that the abstract concept of thenumber linecontains a critical vulnerability. Since hackers cannot interact directly with it, it is unclear how such a 'vulnerability' could ever be exploited. Even more strangely, the problem is apparently specific to a particular value on the number line ("the whole number between two and four") which needs patching to make it safe. Since individual numbers have no special status on the number line, it would be odd for one in particular to be problematic.

Since a fixed piece of software can beexpressedas a (very large) integer, a security vulnerability could be considered to be its corresponding value on the number line.

Cueballimplies that people should avoid even naming the aforementioned number, referring to it as "the integer between two and four", "half six" or "baker's two". The first two euphemisms are simply correct mathematics (an integer being a whole number), whereas a "baker's two" borrows from the phrase "baker's dozen". A dozen means 12, but a baker's dozen is 13, with the reason commonly given being that bakers add one more to a dozen in case they have baked a dud or short measure. By the same logic, a baker's two is one more than two. Fictional variations of baker's dozens were previously explored in3092: Baker's Units. Cueball's suggestion that this only applies to counting small numbers makes no sense: however large the number you wish to count, you will first have to count through all the smaller numbers in order to get to it.
