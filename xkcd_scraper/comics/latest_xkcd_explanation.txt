This comic was published about a week aftertrade talks collapsed between the United States and Canada, leading to both levying heavy tariffs on each other.

Cueball's arms and legs are one part of a closed circulatory loop. The body sends oxygen-rich blood out around the limbs and receives oxygen-depleted blood in return. In isolation, that looks like a lopsided "deal." The body keeps sending high-value goods (O₂) and getting low-value goods (CO₂) back. But this is not a market failure; it's the entire point of having a circulatory system. Punishing one limb hurts the whole body.

The joke maps onto a recurring argument in U.S. trade politics: that a bilateral trade deficit is inherently a loss.Donald Trumphas repeatedly made versions of this claim. For instance, hetweeted in 2019that the U.S. was "losing, for many years, 600 to 800 Billion Dollars a year on Trade," and more recently cited Canada's trade surplus with the U.S. as part of a $200 billion figure the U.S. was supposedly "losing" to its northern neighbor.Commentary at the timenoted that this framing treats the world as a zero-sum game, where anything not made domestically counts as a loss.

Economists across the spectrum have pushed back on this accounting. Boston University economist Tarek Alexander Hassanwrotethat a trade deficit "doesn't mean the US is losing money." It just means foreigners are sending more goods than they're receiving in return. Peterson Institute fellow Gary Hufbauerquippedthat running a deficit with a country is "like saying that I lose $25,000 a year to Albertsons, where I do my shopping."

The "body sends oxygen, leg sends CO₂" setup is a fairly good stand-in for the standard rebuttal to deficit alarmism: a trade deficit is just the flip side of a capital account surplus. Commentators have pointed out that a country's trade deficit is largely the mirror image of foreigners wanting to hold its assets: you can't run an investment surplus and a trade surplus at the same time, which is why calling for both is incoherent. Economist Noah Smith hasmade a similar pointabout the misdiagnosis at the heart of deficit hawkery, framing the reflexive assumption that a negative trade-balance number means the country is "losing money" the same way a business loses money as the root confusion, when in fact, as with the circulatory system, one leg of the flow existing to balance the other is not evidence of exploitation.

The title text swaps in a second, more advanced trade-policy pun: "comparative advantage," Ricardo's classical argument that both parties gain from trade even if one party is better at everything because specializing in one's relatively-best activity still beats self-sufficiency, versus "competitive advantage," simply being better at a given task in absolute terms. The arm's threat that legs may be relatively better suited to running, but arms could physically overpower them by controlling the oxygen supply is a punchline version of an argument that has shown up almost verbatim in real trade-policy debates: that a country's specialization gains from comparative advantage are conditional on the dominant power's forbearance, and national-security or industrial-dominance concerns justify overriding them. Cueball ironically uses his other limbs (also a net consumer of oxygen, but supposedly justified by the fact that arms are good at "swinging hammers," and therefore considered somehow more valid recipients of oxygenated lifeblood) to threaten the legs (whose service to the body seem to be far less valued).

Peter Navarro, formerly Trump's senior trade advisor, has acknowledged[actual citation needed]the textbook logic that, under the Ricardian model, if two countries trade freely and fairly, each works to its comparative advantage and the collective pie gets bigger, while simultaneously arguing that the U.S. shouldn't accept the resulting trade pattern, framing reliance on other countries as a national security threat that justifies tariffs, regardless of the efficiency case. He has also explicitly cast global supply-chain dominance as a power struggle to be won, rather than a specialization to be accepted, writing that a rival power's control over a resource is being used to "dominate global commerce and project state power," and describing the U.S. response in the same competitive terms the arm uses: building alternatives fast enough to "gain a competitive advantage." That's structurally the same move as the title text's arm: conceding the comparative-advantage math, then arguing the dominant party should use its leverage anyway before the other side's natural advantage sticks.
