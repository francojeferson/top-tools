Some earlier cosmological theories, dating from at least classical times, and current up to the renaissance period, were not based on concepts of gravitationally bound orbits. They held that the Sun, Moon, and planets were attached to a series of concentric transparentcrystal spheres, which rotated around either the Earth (in geocentric models) or the Sun (in helocentric models), producing the motion of these bodies in the sky. The most distant is that of the 'fixed stars' — the ones that do not appear to move relative to each other. These cosmologies were gradually overturned by gravitational models, with early ideas proposed by Kepler and a more comprehensive system developed by Newton.

TheVoyager probeshave been traveling outward from the Solar System, withVoyager 1recentlyreaching a distance of approximately one light-day; it is the most distant space probe launched from Earth. As it sets new distance records, it repeatedly crosses various boundaries which could be considered the edge of the Solar System, though the definition of such an 'edge' is subjective (as has been discussed in previous comics, such as1189: Voyager 1). In this comic, it has encountered an objective edge, by crashing into the sphere of the fixed stars, which, surprisingly, turns out to exist after all. The impact appears to have destroyed the probe. Billowing dust clouds around the impact point also suggest that rather than being vacuum-filled, the crystal sphere must be filled withsome sort of fluidwith which the dust interacts rather than being ejected inertially.

The title text pokes further fun at the arbitrariness of the Solar System's boundaries by re-casting the crystal sphere as a "crystal sphere zone", making what would otherwise be an objective boundary subjective and fuzzy again. This is a reference to the "crying wolf" period in 2013 ofrepeatedVoyager 1articlesclaimingthat it hadleft the solar system.
