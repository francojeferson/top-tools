CueballandMeganare browsing through alist of potentially habitable planets.Planetary habitabilitydepends on many factors, including having a temperature range in which water is a liquid, having a solid surface, and having an atmosphere.

The first exoplanet Megan looks at, despite being in the habitable zone, turns out to have an unfavorable surface temperature. While the habitable zone, in theory, should account for an ideal surface temperature (thus its alternate name of Goldilocks zone, in reference to the fable's "not too hot, not too cold, just right"), it is far from a guarantee. One example lies in Venus, within our own star system: Venus iswithin the Sun's habitable zone, but its runaway greenhouse effect (among other factors) have left ita terrible placewith a surface temperature hot enough to melt steel.

The second exoplanet appears to be a water planet, which is promising for habitability. Unfortunately, its mass relative to its size prohibits a solid surface. Cueball identifies this as a "mini-Neptune" - a planet which shares traits with gas giants like Neptune, but smaller in size. Mini-Neptunes were previously mentioned in3103: Exoplanet System.

Megan describes the third exoplanet to catch her eye as "habitable if it has a greenhouse effect". The greenhouse effect, which induces temperature increases, is caused by buildup of greenhouse gasses within the atmosphere. However, Megan then observes that spectroscopy of the mentioned exoplanet has no atmosphere whatsoever, thus prohibiting a greenhouse effect.[citation needed]

The punchline comes when Megan finds a planet with the correct habitable factors. Alas, she is looking at our Earth, which orbits our Sun (which is also called Sol). This is similar to1231: Habitable Zone, where an astronomer fails to realize that he is looking at (a reflection of) Earth before he gets excited about its habitability.

The title text expands on this joke by saying that the observatory is looking at sunsets instead of planets. It's not clear if this is being done with the observatory's instruments or just with people's eyes, which act spectroscopically since we can see different colors. Light at sunset (or sunrise) travels through more of the atmosphere before reaching the ground, so more of its blue color gets scattered andwe see more red and orange colors— making it colorful, and pretty.
