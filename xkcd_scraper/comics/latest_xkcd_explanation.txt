This comic suggests that the annual meeting of theInternational Bureau of Weights and Measures(BIPM) would, instead of saying "hours" or "minutes", ask the attendees to use the precise form of measurement the BIPM uses to define the second, thehyperfine transition emission frequency of cesium-133. Just one second takes a very large number of these transitions, namely 9,192,631,770 of them, so transition counts for events at a conference are correspondingly even larger. The numbers of transitionsCueballmentions correspond to two hours for the opening session and an hour and a half for lunch.

The attendees are supposed to keep track of the time by each of them monitoring an individual cesium atom in a bag. In reality, acesium-basedatomic clockrequires a large amount of specialized machinery, and the cesium atoms are cooled to nearabsolute zeroand suspended in a vacuum. Cesium is highly reactive, so a cesium atom placed in a bag would be likely to react quickly with other materials.

In practical terms, cesium is ubiquitous in matter on Earth, albeit at very low concentration in most places. A bag made of any normal material is almost certain to contain at least one atom of cesium (in ionic form), especially if it has collected any dust made from rocky material. For example, a dust particle 100 μm in diameter is just large enough to be visible by most people. Assuming a sphere, that's a volume of about 5 × 10-13m3. Granite has a density of about 2.7 × 103kg/m3, giving a particle mass of about 1.35 × 10-6g. The abundance of cesium-133 in granite is about 1 ppm, giving a mass of cesium of 1.35 × 10-12g. Cs-133 has an atomic mass of about 133 g/mol (obviously!), so that particle has about 1.0 × 10-14mol of cesium, or about 6 × 109atoms.

The title text alludes to the common tradition of presenting retiring committee members (in this case, the chairperson, shorted to chair) with a gold-plated award to commemorate their work; this is often a watch or clock, which continues the theme of time-keeping. In the comic, they have decided to present a gold-plated cesium atom instead. While plating single atoms is not a practical option, it could refer tocesium auride, a chemical compound of cesium and gold. If it were feasible, 'plating' a single cesium atom would be ridiculously cheap as far as material costs are concerned, since individual atoms have a very low surface area, though themethodof properly isolating and combining the gold and cesium atoms might contribute far higher costs.
