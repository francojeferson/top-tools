This comic depicts four space missions which were supposedly rejected, because each would be a bad idea.

The “Jupiter Rover” mission depicts aMars rover-like vehicle onJupiter. As the rover is a wheeled vehicle, it can only operate on a body with a solid surface such as Mars. Jupiter is agas giantand it is unknown whether it has a solid surface, as none of our instruments could measure deep enough into its atmosphere. Even if it has a solid core, it would be under a layer ofmetallic hydrogen, which a rover would also struggle to survive in. In addition, Jupiter has winds in excess of 1,000 km/h, atmospheric pressures exceeding 1 million atm, and temperatures exceeding 24,000 K; any rover made with current technology and materials would be destroyed upon deployment before even reaching the metallic hydrogen layer. TheGalileo probe, specifically designed to penetrate the Jovian atmosphere, was destroyed when the atmospheric pressure was approximately 24 atm.

The “Voyager 2 Orbiter” mission would send a probe to orbitVoyager 2, which is itself a space probe. As Voyager 2 was built on Earth, its size, shape, and features were already well known to NASA before it was launched into space, making this mission pointless; it would also interfere with some of Voyager’s own still-ongoing measurements of its current environment. Orbiting Voyager 2 would also be a non-trivial task. Voyager 2 has a mass of 722 kg and a minor axis of 2.5 m. Escape velocity for any unpowered orbit would be 0.68 meters per hour, and the relative mass of the ‘orbiter’ is likely to form abinary systembetween the two objects. Trivial fluctuations in velocity would lead the co-orbiting pair to be unstable; these are unavoidable due to the asymmetry of the Voyager probe. Powered orbits would require non-trivial mass forstation keeping. In any case this mission would require somehow catching up with the second-furthest man-made object there is, which would have five or more decades of head start on any mission yet to be launched. Randall haspreviously discussedsending a mission to Voyager 1.

The “Crewed Pluto Flyby” mission would send astronauts to pass byPlutoin order to collect scientific data about the dwarf planet. However, the mission as depicted would have the astronauts pass Pluto and continue out of the solar system, apparently with no way of returning to Earth. In addition, current timeframes for a Pluto flyby, includinggravitational assistswith Jupiter and Saturn as shown, are on the order of a decade. Sustaining astronauts on such missions for years requires on the order of 1 kg/person/day.[actual citation needed]The entire mass of theNew Horizonsprobe, the only spacecraft thus far to perform a Pluto flyby, would be required each year for each astronaut. Due to the limits of theTsiolkovsky rocket equation, this exceeds the combined payloads of all space missions to date.

The “Neptune Core Return” mission would somehow extract the solid core deep beneathNeptune’s gaseous atmosphere (possibly a pun about takingcore samples), then tow it back to Earth. The core of Neptune is estimated to be on the order of the size of the Earth, so any such towing out of Neptune’sgravity wellwould require impractical amounts of energy. The atmospheric pressure near the Neptunian core is estimated to exceed 100,000 atm, meaning it would require significant advances in technology for a probe to survive descent to Neptune’s core. If Neptune’s core were somehow returned to Earth, the collision woulddestroy all life on our planet. Space probeshavereturned materialfrom solar system bodies beyond the Moon, but only in amounts less than 200 g.

The title text claims that the Voyager 2 orbiter project was scrapped because the probe actually prefers to be alone. Voyager does not have any such preferences, as it is not sentient[citation needed](althougha successor to itbecame so in the filmStar Trek: The Motion Picture). This maybe a reference to Al Worden's experience of enjoying being alone in the lunar orbiter during the Apollo 15 mission, which Randall has used as a closing remark ofa What if? article.
