Nuclear fusionis a process in which lighternuclei of atomsfuse into heavier ones. Because nuclei are positively charged, overcoming their mutual repulsion and bringing them together requires colliding them at high speed. This can be done directly with particle accelerators, but if the goal is to do the process on a scale that allows net energy release, accelerators aren't a workable solution because they require a great deal of energy input. Another approach is to bring the nuclei to a very high temperature (tens to hundreds of millions of degrees Celsius) so they're moving randomly at high speeds, and will occasionally collide in such a way as to fuse.Ponytailis extremely surprised atCueballandMegan's claim that they have built a workingcold fusionmachine, which would create such reactions at room temperature, and even more so that it was "easy", because this is a long-hypothesised technology that has remainedstubbornly unachievable. Had they actually managed to do this, it would certainly put them in contention for theNobel Prizethey are expecting. (Regardless of such a breakthrough, they would still need to go through anomination and selectionprocess rather than just receiving a prize right away.)

It turns out that they have mistaken an atomic diagram of adeuterium–tritium fusionfor a physical picture of a machine used to create such a reaction, replacing the particles involved with fasteners that have a similar appearance to the depictions in the diagram. They have interpretedprotons, illustrated by a circle with a plus sign (+), asPhillips head screws;electrons, marked with a minus sign (−), asslotted screws(commonly known as flat head); andneutrons, denoted by an empty circle, asrivets. They seem not to have wondered how a box of screws could create cold fusion, or why no-one else has done it before if it's that simple, or for that matter so much as tested their creation to ensure it actually performed the requested task before coming to Ponytail and asking about their Nobel Prize.

Whereas high temperatures are (so far) essential for fusion technology, rivets are usually applied at room temperature, possessing thin metal that can be splayed easily with a hammer and shaped spreading tools – where one side is struck while the other rests in a shaped spreading anvil – or using single-sided variants for blind fastening in situations where only one side can be accessed, using dedicated tools that pull the buried end back toward the accessible end, thus squashing the material between these extremities to create the spreading effect (e.g. pop rivets or rivnuts). Working with hot rivets (i.e. those that are heated to the point of being malleable so they can be splayed to lock them into the joint that they are being used to fasten) is a markedly more skilled and hazardous task than room-temperature ones. The title text plays off this as a continuation of Cueball and Megan's mistake, having one of them point out that the additional hassle of using hot rivets – such as one would employ for heavy-duty industrial steelwork – would make construction of their 'device' far more difficult than doing it at room temperature.
