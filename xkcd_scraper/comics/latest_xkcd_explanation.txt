Voyager 1is a space probe launched in 1977, almost 50 years before this comic, aimed at operating at the distance of the outer planets and potentially onward towards interstellar space — which it andother probeshave so far been very successful in accomplishing. Like other interstellar/outer-planet probes, it usesradioisotope thermoelectric generatorsrather than solar panels, which don't work well or reliably enough on such extended missions at such a great distance from the Sun. These generators are powered by radioactive materials which emit heat as they decay, which is, in turn, used to generate electricity. These generators can provide continuous power for decades with no maintenance, no moving parts and no access to sunlight, making them ideal for space missions such as these. The main disadvantages of such generators are the high cost and the presence of very hazardous materials, both of which are of comparatively minor importance in a groundbreaking unmanned space mission such as Voyager.Randallhaspreviously referred to these generators, calling them "unrealistic" because they're so useful.

While such generators are long-lived, the gradual decay of the nuclear fuel means that their power output declines over time. Half a century into the mission, Voyager 1's available power hasfallen considerably. In order to maximize the value of the mission, scientists atNASAhave turned off more and more of Voyager's scientific instruments, so that the remaining instruments can still have power. This requires making a decision as to which instruments provide the most scientific value, in relation to the amount of power they require.

In this strip, they debate whether to turn off thePlasma Wave Subsystem(an instrument that measures electron density in interstellar space) or themagnetometer(an instrument that measures magnetic field strength). As of the date of this comic posting (September 2026), this is correct: these are the only twoscientific instruments currently activeon Voyager 1.

In this comic's reality, it's revealed there is something else drawing power: Greg has apparently installed "LED underlighting" on Voyager 1. Underlighting, orunderglow, is a decorative system used on the underside of automobiles. It has little practical function, and is entirely for appearance. Such a system would be a ridiculous waste of weight and power on a space probe, since a) the probe exists for scientific purposes, not aesthetics,[citation needed]and b) there's no one around to see the probe in any case.

Greg refuses to turn off the underlighting, claiming that it would make humans "look uncool in front of aliens". This argument isn't quite as ridiculous as it would initially appear. Voyager probes do, in fact, carry theVoyager Golden Record, which was designed to convey the basics of human culture, language and science, meaning that the possibility of the probe being discovered by aliens in the future was given actual consideration. However, even if the probe were discovered, it's highly unlikely that the lighting would still be active, and also unlikely that the aliens would be impressed by the lighting, even if it was. The lights might make the probe easier to spot in space, but it's unclear how useful that would be, and Greg is clearly only interested in the aesthetic value.

The title text mentions that Greg has added even more car-like 'upgrade' features to Voyager 1, including a "stupidlaser show" (lasers in space do not scatter due to the absence of particles in a vacuum, making an Earth-style laser show very difficult to view in space) and a "uselesssound system" (sound does not travel in space), which were turned off only after a "huge ordeal" which nobody wants to repeat. The suggestion is that, while Greg's ideas are pointless and wasteful, he's so adamant about them that the others don't care to have a repeat of the argument.
