This comic demonstrates a relatively linear relationship between the length of variousparticle acceleratorsand the collision energy they are capable of producing. It then adds an outlier data point for theDaytona International Speedway, a track for car races, which suggests it is much more efficient for its size than the accelerators. This is not a particularly meaningful comparison, since the two are completely different things.[citation needed]

eV stands forelectronvolt, a non-SI unit of energy. It is typically used inparticle physics, where exceedingly small and light particles are accelerated to exceedingly high velocities approaching the speed of light, thus boosting their energy; it is also sometimes used as a unit of mass throughE=mc2, such as in2186: Dark Matterand2035: Dark Matter Candidates. Trying to increase the energy as much as possible to create collisions that allow us to probe ever deeper in subatomic physics leads to ever-larger machines, some having circumferences ofdozens of kilometers, necessary because particles' energy losses are smaller with larger, gentler curves. This also makes accelerators far more expensive.

Judging by collision energy alone, a race car is a far more space-efficient way to cause a collision than a particle accelerator. At first glance, it may seem remarkable that a simple raceway produces collisions with energies so much higher than those of the various particle colliders, by about 10 orders of magnitude. It is important to note that the comparison needs to take into account the masses of the colliding objects. A vehicle might have a mass of 1000 kg or more, with a currentGeneration 6vehicle specified for a minimum operational mass of 1542 kg. The Large Hadron Collider operates on particles ranging from protons (1 atomic mass unit, 1.66 x 10-27kg) to lead atom nuclei (204 to 208 AMUs, 3.388 x 10-25to 3.45 x 10-25kg). Race cars are thus more than 1027times as massive as the particles accelerated by the LHC, and therefore have higher collision energies despite their much, much slower speeds. Particle colliders do, however, provide much higher energy-divided-by-mass values. Racetracks can be shorter than the largest accelerators but, beyond a certain point, this isn't hugely relevant to the collision speed, which has more to do with the capabilities of the cars.

Some particle accelerators have been designed for collisions between matter andantimatter, such as betweenelectrons and positronsorprotons and antiprotons. Introducing antimatter circling in the opposite direction allows more energy to be put into the collision products because the original particles are destroyed (at least in theory; withbaryons, it is often the case that only some of thequarks/antiquarks annihilate), so the equivalent energy of their rest mass—and, more significantly, their remaining kinetic energy after the collision—does not detract from the energy budget available to create collision products. This does not matter as much as people think it does, but it could lead to audience excitement because of pure energy (light) that would be emitted.

Introducing a hypothetical antimatter race car going in the wrong direction would not only result in even more carnage but in a huge explosion as well. TheHiroshima atomic blastreleased energy equivalent toless than a gram of matter, so a matter-antimatter annihilation of 1000 kg race cars would be more than a million times larger — more than sufficient to excite the particles of all spectators present to the point of their complete destruction.
